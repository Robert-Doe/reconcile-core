Flow ⊃ Phrasing: the big two

Ninety percent of everyday HTML nesting comes down to one relationship: phrasing content is a subset of flow content. That's the modern, precise version of the old "inline vs block" intuition.

Flow content — the body's default

The spec's own summary is blunt: most elements used in the body are flow content. If you're wondering "can this go in a <div>, a <section>, a <figure>?", the answer is usually "yes, if it's flow" — because those containers accept flow content.

Phrasing content — text and its inline markup

Phrasing content is, in the spec's words, "the text of the document, as well as elements that mark up that text at the intra-paragraph level." Think <span>, <em>, <strong>, <a>, <code>, <br>, and raw Text nodes. Runs of phrasing content are what form paragraphs (page 6).

💡 The "why can't I nest a div in a p" answer
Because <p>'s content model is phrasing, and <div> is flow-but-not-phrasing. The parser even enforces this: open a <div> inside a <p> and the parser auto-closes the <p> first (a tree-construction rule — tie this to the main course's Module 02 / Track B foster-parenting family).

Categorize to see the overlap

Check a few: span (flow+phrasing), div (flow only), img (flow+phrasing+embedded). The presence or absence of "phrasing" is the block/inline line.

Why a defender cares

Sanitizer allowlists are content-category allowlists in disguise. When you configure DOMPurify (or the native Sanitizer) to allow "b i em strong a code span", you are permitting a hand-picked slice of phrasing content. When you allow "p ul ol li blockquote", you're adding some flow containers. Understanding the categories lets you reason about your allowlist as a coherent set ("phrasing + a few safe flow blocks") instead of an ad-hoc tag list — and spot the dangerous outliers (embedded content, interactive content, transparent elements) that don't belong in user HTML at all.
