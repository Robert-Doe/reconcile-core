Module 18 · where defenses fail

Gap Analysis of Existing Defenses

A defense's gaps are more useful to a researcher than its strengths. Here is an honest accounting of where each pillar leaks, so your intervention can aim at a real, unsolved seam rather than reinventing a solved one.

Gap 1 — Encoding is only as good as its context awareness

  • Wrong-context encoding: HTML-encoding a value that lands in a JS or URL context does nothing (Module 06/08). Correctness depends on the developer choosing the right encoder at every sink — a per-call human decision that fails at scale.
  • Not idempotent under mutation: encoding operates on output strings; it doesn't defend the re-parse inequality of mXSS (Module 13).
  • Nothing for DOM sinks: server-side encoding can't see client-side source→sink flows (Module 09).

Seam: automatic, context-inferring encoding that is correct by construction at every sink — and stable under re-serialization.

Gap 2 — CSP is powerful but widely misdeployed and partially bypassable

  • 'unsafe-inline' everywhere: a large fraction of real CSPs include it, neutering coverage of mechanisms 1–2 (Module 17).
  • Allowlist bypasses: host allowlists fall to JSONP endpoints, open redirects on allowed hosts, and CDNs hosting bypass gadgets.
  • DOM sinks survive: CSP doesn't stop innerHTML DOM-XSS unless paired with Trusted Types.
  • Adoption friction: nonce plumbing through templates and third-party scripts is nontrivial; many sites never reach a strict policy.
  • Absent entirely: countless apps send no CSP at all — the exact default this lab models.

Seam: capability enforcement that doesn't depend on the server shipping a correct header, and that resists allowlist-gadget bypasses.

Gap 3 — Trusted Types has the right shape but limited reach

  • Browser support: Chromium-centric; no enforcement in non-Chromium engines today, so it can't be a sole control.
  • Rewire cost: every DOM-sink call site must route through a policy; legacy codebases and third-party widgets resist this.
  • Scope: guards DOM sinks (mechanism 4) and script URLs, but not server-reflected HTML (mechanism 1 in the initial response) or attribute-context break-out on the server side.

Seam: a portable, in-browser fallback that provides TT-like "strings can't reach sinks" guarantees where TT is unavailable, with low rewrite cost.

Gap 4 — Sanitizers chase an ever-moving mXSS frontier

  • Mutation gadgets recur: each new parser/namespace interaction can yield a new bypass; safety is a maintenance treadmill (Module 13).
  • Config drift: allowlists of tags/attrs and namespace handling must track platform growth; a stale config is a hole.
  • Input-time misuse: sanitizing once at input rather than at each output/context reintroduces second-order bugs (Module 10).

Seam: sanitization with a provable stability property (idempotent under re-serialization) rather than an empirically-patched blocklist of gadgets.

Gap 5 — Impact controls are orthogonal and often missing

  • HttpOnly, re-auth, SRI, iframe isolation reduce impact (Module 15/16) but don't prevent execution, and each is independently forgettable.
  • Card-skimming (Magecart/BA) shows that egress and secret isolation are underused compared to execution-focused controls.

Seam: a coherent "assume execution, deny egress + isolate secrets" architecture with a usable default.

Synthesis: the shape of a strong intervention

The most defensible PhD contributions in this space tend to:

  1. Target a specific seam above (ideally mechanism 5 idempotence, or deployability of capability controls) rather than "better filtering."
  2. Offer a provable property (idempotence, non-interference, fail-closed) instead of an empirical blocklist.
  3. Be deployable where current controls aren't — e.g. a purely client-side shim for no-CSP legacy apps, or a non-Chromium Trusted-Types polyfill with real enforcement.
  4. Come with an evaluation harness (Module 19 corpus + a differential parse/serialize oracle) that measures coverage against mechanisms, bypass rate, performance, and compatibility.

Checkpoint

  1. Pick one seam and argue why it is unsolved, citing the specific module evidence.
  2. What distinguishes a "provable property" defense from a "blocklist" defense, and why do reviewers prefer the former?
  3. What must your evaluation measure to be credible?
